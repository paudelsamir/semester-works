## **1. Introduction**

- *Management Theory* → A lens to understand organisational structure, behaviour, and performance. Helps managers ask *why* and *how* an organisation works.
- Evolution grouped into three broad schools:
    - *Classical Theory* → Efficiency, structure, formal rules.
    - *Neo-Classical Theory* → Human factor, relationships, behaviour.
    - *Modern Theory* → Systems, contingencies, quantitative tools.

---

## **2. Classical Theory of Management (organization as machine)** - Taylor, Fayol, Weber.

*Focus* (efficiency and output) → Organisation structure, scientific methods, formal principles.

### **2.1 Scientific Management — F.W. Taylor (*Taylorism*) - one best way focus**

- *Goal* → Improve productivity through scientific, research-backed methods.

- *Four Principles*:
    1. *Science, not rule‑of‑thumb* → Develop a standard method for each task.
    2. *Scientific selection & training* → Choose workers scientifically, then train and develop them.
    3. *Co‑operation* → Management and workers heartily cooperate to ensure work follows the science.
    4. *Equal division of work & responsibility* → Management takes work it's better fitted for (planning, supervision); workers focus on execution.

- *Example* → Instead of letting each worker decide their own way to shovel coal, Taylor's method determined the optimal shovel size, movements, and rest periods — output jumped significantly.

### **2.2 Administrative Management — Henry Fayol (*Fayolism*) - overall structure focus**

- *Focus* → Managerial functions and broad principles for the whole organisation.
- ***Five Functions*** (covered in Chapter 2) → Planning, Organising, Commanding, Co‑ordinating, Controlling.
- ***14 Principles*** (covered in Chapter 2): Division of work, Authority‑Responsibility, Discipline, Unity of Command, Unity of Direction, Subordination of individual interest, Remuneration, Centralisation, Scalar Chain, Order, Equity, Stability, Initiative, Esprit de Corps.

### **2.3 Bureaucracy — Max Weber - strict hierarchy focus**

- *Concept* → Ideal organisation based on *legal‑rational authority*.
- *Features*:
    - Clear division of labour.
    - Hierarchical structure.
    - Formal rules & procedures.
    - Impersonal relationships.
    - Employment based on technical qualifications.
- *Example* → Government civil service: positions, promotions, and duties defined by written rules, not personal favours.

---

### **2.4 Limitations of Classical Theory**

- *Ignores human factor* → Treats workers as machines; no attention to social/psychological needs.
- *Closed system view* → Assumes organisation is isolated from its external environment.
- *Oversimplified motivation* → Relies solely on economic rewards; overlooks non‑monetary motivators (recognition, belonging).
- *Based on limited observations* → Principles derived from managerial experience, not rigorous research.
- *Ambiguous & contradictory principles* → For example, Unity of Command vs. Specialist advice.
- *Excessive rigidity* → Strict adherence to rules kills individual initiative and creativity.
- *Example of limitation* → A factory strictly following Taylor's stop‑watch methods may face worker resistance and strikes because it ignores fatigue and monotony.

## **3. Neo‑Classical Theory (Human Relations & Behavioural Science theory)**

*Focus people and motivation (worker satisfaction)*

*Shift* → From "organisation as a machine" to **"organisation as a social system"**.

### **3.1 Human Relations Theory — Elton Mayo (*Hawthorne Studies*) - employees work harder when they feel valued, noticed.**

- *Origin* → Series of experiments at Western Electric's Hawthorne plant.
- *Key Findings*:
    - *Social factors* (group belonging, attention, recognition) significantly affect productivity.
    - *Informal groups* exist within formal structures and influence behaviour.
    - *Psychological & social needs* matter as much as economic incentives.
    - Workers respond positively when managers show concern for their well‑being — *Hawthorne Effect*.
- *Example* → When lighting was improved for a test group, output rose; but it also rose for the control group because workers felt special just by being part of the study.

### **3.2 Behavioural Science Theory**

- *Broader* → Draws on psychology, sociology, anthropology.
- *Key Contributor — Abraham Maslow*: *Hierarchy of Needs*.
    - Physiological → Safety → Social → Esteem → Self‑actualisation.
    - Managers must address the level of need currently most pressing for an employee.
- *Other contributors*: Douglas McGregor (Theory X & Theory Y), Frederick Herzberg (Two‑factor theory).
- *Core Ideas*:
    - Motivation is complex; no single formula.
    - Reconciliation needed between individual goals and organisational goals.
    - Teamwork and participation boost commitment.

### **3.3 Contribution & Limitation of Neo‑Classical Theory**

*Contribution*:

- Brought the *human element* to centre stage.
- Recognised the role of informal organisation, leadership, communication, and participation.
- Paved the way for modern HR practices.

*Limitation*:

- Sometimes seen merely as "classical theory with human insights attached".
- Overemphasis on human relations can lead to neglect of structure and productivity.
- Does not provide a complete framework for complex, dynamic environments — later addressed by contingency theory.

## **4. Modern Management Theories (***Focus adaptability to changes and systems***) - quantitative, system, contingency (it depends), decision**

### **4.1 Quantitative Approach (Management Science)**

*Focus* → Apply mathematical and statistical methods to solve management problems.

- *Branches*:
    - *Management Science*: Uses models like PERT, CPM, linear programming, simulation for planning and decision‑making.
        - *Example*: CPM (Critical Path Method) used to plan a construction project — identifies the longest stretch of dependent activities and optimises timeline.
    - *Operations Management*: Focuses on efficient production processes, quality control, forecasting, inventory management.
        - *Example*: A restaurant uses queuing theory to determine how many servers are needed during peak hours to minimise customer wait time.
    - *Management Information System (MIS)*: Collects, stores real‑time data; enables managers to monitor performance, generate reports, and make informed decisions.
        - *Example*: Dashboard showing live sales data, inventory levels, and staff productivity.

### **4.2 System Theory**

*Core Idea* → Organisation as an *open system* made of interdependent parts. interconnected like change in one dept impacts every other dept.

- *Components*:
    - *Inputs* → Raw materials, capital, human resources, information.
    - *Transformation Process* → Production, operations, employee activities.
    - *Outputs* → Products, services, profits, wages.
    - *Feedback* → Information on outputs used to adjust inputs/processes.
- *Key Insight* → **Change in one subsystem affects others. Managers must see the whole, not fragments.**
- *Example* → A dip in product quality (output) triggers feedback; investigation reveals raw material defect (input) → purchasing department adjusts supplier criteria.

### **4.3 Contingency Theory**

*Core Idea* → **There is *no single best way* to manage. The optimal management style *depends on the situation*.**

- *Variables*: Organisation size, technology, environmental uncertainty, task routineness, individual differences.
- *Manager's task* → Diagnose the situation and choose the most fitting approach.
- *Example*:
    - A stable manufacturing unit may do well with a mechanistic, rule‑based structure.
    - A creative advertising agency in a fast‑changing market thrives with an organic, flexible, participative style.
- *Difference from earlier theories* → Replaces universal principles with "It depends."

### **4.4 Decision Theory**

*Core Idea* → Management is essentially *decision‑making*. The organisation is viewed as a decision‑making unit.

- *Key Thinker*: Herbert A. Simon.
- *Concepts*:
    - *Rational decision‑making model*: Identify problem → generate alternatives → evaluate → choose optimal solution.
    - *Bounded Rationality*: Humans have limited information, time, and cognitive capacity. They seek *satisfactory* rather than *optimal* decisions — "satisficing".
    - *Administrative Man*: Simon's realistic model of the decision‑maker, in contrast to the "Economic Man" who is perfectly rational.
- *Example* → A manager choosing a new software vendor does not evaluate *every* option worldwide. Instead, she gathers a reasonable number of quotes, compares key features, and picks one that meets the budget and requirements — a "good enough" decision.

## **5. Emerging Management Concepts (workforce diversity, outsourcing, knowledge mgmt, learning orgs)**

### **5.1 Workforce Diversity**

- *Definition* → A workforce composed of individuals with differences in *skills, age, gender, religion, culture, education, economic status*, etc.
- *Types*:
    - *Internal* → Inherent traits from birth (age, gender, race, physical ability).
    - *External* → Acquired through life (education, religion, experience, marital status).
    - *Organisational* → Earned within the job (role, hierarchy, employment status, union membership).
    - *Worldview* → How an individual interprets global events (shaped by all the above).
- *Importance*:
    - Brings *new ideas & perspectives*.
    - Fuels *innovation & productivity*.
    - Helps serve *diverse customer bases* better.
    - Improves *decision‑making* by reducing groupthink.
- *Example* → A product design team with members from different age groups and cultures creates a gadget that appeals to both young tech‑savvy users and older customers seeking simplicity.

### **5.2 Outsourcing**

- *Definition* → Contracting out a business function or process to an external third‑party supplier, rather than handling it in‑house.
- *Advantages*:
    - *Cost savings* → Access to lower labour or operational costs.
    - *Increased efficiency & productivity* → Focus on core competencies while experts handle non‑core work.
    - *Improved quality* → Specialist providers bring advanced skills and technology.
- *Disadvantages*:
    - *Loss of control* → Dependency on vendor's performance and timelines.
    - *Data security risks* → Sensitive information shared externally.
    - *Communication issues* → Time zone, language, and cultural barriers.
- *Example* → A software company outsources its customer support call centre to a specialised firm, reducing overhead costs but requiring strong contracts to protect user data.

### **5.3 Knowledge Management**

- *Definition* → Process of creating, capturing, storing, and sharing organisational knowledge so it's available for *everyone*, rather than trapped in a few heads (*knowledge bottlenecks*).
- *Types of Knowledge*:
    - *Explicit Knowledge* → Easily codified and shared: manuals, SOPs, handbooks, policies.
    - *Tacit Knowledge* → Personal, experience‑based, hard to articulate: design intuition, leadership skills, troubleshooting know‑how.
- *Importance*:
    - Captures *valuable information* before employees leave or retire.
    - Allows *easy access* to knowledge resources → faster problem‑solving.
    - Cultivates a culture where *knowledge is valued and shared*.
    - Treats *knowledgeable employees as assets*, not just tools.
- *Example* → A senior welder nearing retirement records video demonstrations and writes tip sheets capturing his tacit technique; new hires use these to reduce training time and avoid mistakes.

### **5.4 Learning Organization**

- *Definition* → An organisation that fosters a culture of *continuous learning and knowledge creation* at all levels, embedding learning into its DNA to adapt, innovate, and thrive.
- *Characteristics* (Peter Senge's model):
    - *Building a Shared Vision* → A common goal that inspires collective commitment and creative thinking.
    - *Personal Mastery* → Individuals continuously deepen their skills and strive for excellence.
    - *Mental Models* → Surfacing and challenging deeply ingrained assumptions and biases that shape how we see the world.
    - *Team Learning* → Teams share insights and collaborate; the group's intelligence exceeds the sum of individual IQs.
    - *Systems Thinking* → Seeing the organisation as an interconnected whole, understanding how parts influence each other.
- *Example* → A hospital regularly conducts "after‑action reviews" where surgical teams openly discuss what went well and what didn't, without blame — this loop becomes a source of continuous improvement in patient care.
