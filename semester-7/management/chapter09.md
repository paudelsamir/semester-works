## **1. Concept of Communication**

- *Communication* → The process of exchanging information and ideas between individuals, groups, or organizations.
- It is the glue that holds planning, decision‑making, problem‑solving, and coordination together.
- Takes many forms: *verbal*, *written*, *non‑verbal*; via face‑to‑face, phone, email, social media.

*Understanding* → Communication is not just speaking; it is ensuring that the message is *received and understood* as intended.

---

## **2. Communication Structure (Flows & Direction)**

*The organisation's hierarchy determines how information moves.*

- *Downward Communication*
    - Flows from *higher to lower* levels.
    - *Purpose*: provide job instructions, policies, feedback.
    - *Examples*: official memos, policy manuals, company newsletters, job directives.
    - *Arrow*: Top Management → Middle → Lower
- *Upward Communication*
    - Flows from *lower to higher* levels.
    - *Purpose*: report progress, problems, suggestions, grievances.
    - *Examples*: suggestion boxes, group meetings, appeal/grievance procedures, performance reports.
    - *Arrow*: Workers → Supervisors → Managers → Top
- *Lateral (Horizontal) Communication*
    - Takes place among members at the *same level* or work group.
    - *Purpose*: coordination, problem solving across departments.
    - *Examples*: project team discussions, coordination meetings between marketing and sales managers.
    - *Arrow*: Peer ↔ Peer

---

## **3. Communication Process**

*A step‑by‑step model showing how meaning is transferred from a source to a receiver.*

1. *Sender (Communicator)* → A person with an idea, intention, or information to share.
    
    *Example*: A manager who wants to announce a new deadline.
    
2. *Encoding* → Translating the idea into words, symbols, gestures, or other systematic sets of symbols.
    
    *Example*: The manager writes an email using clear language and bullet points.
    
3. *Medium / Channel* → The carrier of the message.
    
    *Example*: The email, a phone call, a face‑to‑face meeting.
    
4. *Decoding* → Interpretation of the message by the receiver.
    
    *Example*: The employee reads the email and understands the new deadline.
    
5. *Receiver* → The person or group for whom the message is intended.
6. *Feedback* → The receiver's response or reaction, sent back to the sender.
    
    *Example*: The employee replies, "Got it, I'll submit the report by Friday."
    
7. *Noise* → Any interference that distorts the message.
    
    *Example*: A loud office environment during a conversation, unclear language in the email, or a technical glitch in a video call.
    

*Visual summary*:

Sender → Encoding → Message (Channel) → Decoding → Receiver → Feedback (with Noise affecting any stage)

## **4. Types of Communication**

### **4.1 Formal Communication**

- *Officially established* channels by the organisation.
- Follows the chain of command, departmental meetings, official memos.
- *Purpose*: convey important instructions, policies, decisions.
- *Example*: A company‑wide email from the CEO about a merger; a scheduled department meeting with an agenda.

### **4.2 Informal Communication**

- *Unofficial* channels that develop naturally – grapevine, gossip, informal chats.
- Can be *positive* (quick spread of ideas, bonding) or *negative* (rumours, misinformation).
- *Example*: Colleagues discussing a rumour about a new branch opening over coffee; an instant messaging group for a project team.

### **4.3 Interpersonal Communication**

- *Direct, face‑to‑face or group interaction* between individuals.
- Can be oral (conversations, meetings) or written (personal notes, memos).
- *Example*: A one‑on‑one feedback session between a supervisor and an employee; a team brainstorming meeting.

### **4.4 Non‑verbal Communication**

- Communication through *body language*: eye contact, gestures, posture, facial expressions, tone of voice.
- Must be *consistent* with the verbal message. Inconsistency creates confusion or distrust.
- *Example*: Saying "I'm confident about this plan" while avoiding eye contact and slouching signals doubt. A manager nodding and leaning forward while listening signals interest.

## **5. Barriers to Effective Communication**

| **Barrier** | **Description** | **Example** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Filtering* | Sender deliberately manipulates information so the receiver hears only what is favourable. | A subordinate tells the boss only the good sales figures and hides the customer complaints. |
| *Selective Perception* | Receiver filters the message based on his/her own viewpoint, needs, or background; hears what they want to hear. | A manager interprets a neutral comment as criticism because of past experiences. |
| *Information Overload* | Receiving more information than one can process; leads to missing key points. | An employee receiving 200 emails a day overlooks a crucial safety update. |
| *Emotions* | Extreme emotions (anger, excitement, sadness) cloud judgment and interpretation. | An angry employee misreads a simple request as an attack. |
| *Language* | Words with multiple meanings, jargon, technical terms, or ambiguous phrasing. | A doctor using medical jargon with a patient who feels lost and confused. |
| *Communication Apprehension* | Sender's anxiety or tension about communicating, causing hesitation or poor delivery. | A new manager stumbles and speaks unclearly during a presentation due to nervousness. |
| *Politically Correct Communication* | Avoiding words that might insult, but in doing so the message may become vague or lose sincerity. | Over‑sanitised language that hides real feedback, leaving employees unsure of what to improve. |

---

## **6. Enhancing Effective Communication**

*Strategies to overcome barriers and strengthen clarity:*

1. *Encourage Open Communication* → Build a culture where employees feel safe sharing ideas and concerns without fear.
    
    *Example*: Regular open‑door hours where staff can speak freely to senior managers.
    
2. *Use Clear and Concise Language* → Avoid jargon; use simple, direct words.
    
    *Example*: Instead of "We need to leverage our core competencies to optimise verticals," say "Let's use our key strengths to improve each department."
    
3. *Listen Actively* → Give full attention, ask clarifying questions, provide feedback to confirm understanding.
    
    *Example*: Paraphrase the speaker: "So if I understand correctly, you're suggesting a revised timeline. Is that right?"
    
4. *Encourage Face‑to‑Face Communication* → For important or sensitive issues, in‑person meetings build trust and reduce misinterpretation.
    
    *Example*: Delivering a negative performance review in a private, face‑to‑face discussion rather than a cold email.
    
5. *Use Different Communication Channels* → Match the medium to the message and audience (email for records, phone for quick clarification, video for remote teams, instant messaging for urgent pings).
6. *Regularly Check for Understanding* → Don't assume the message was received; ask for a summary or a confirmation.
    
    *Example*: At the end of a briefing, ask: "Can you recap the three key actions you'll take now?"
    
7. *Encourage Feedback* → Allow employees to give input on the communication process itself; use surveys or informal check‑ins to improve.
8. *Provide Training* → Offer workshops on active listening, non‑verbal cues, writing skills, and giving/receiving feedback to sharpen everyone's communication toolkit.
