## **1. Concept of Control System**

- *Controlling* → The process of evaluating and correcting performance to ensure that actual activities match planned activities, and organizational objectives are accomplished.
- It is a *systematic effort* by managers to keep everything on track.
- *Relationship*: Planning sets the direction; controlling ensures you stay on course.

---

## **2. Process of Control**

*Four sequential steps form the control cycle.*

1. *Setting Standards*
    - Standards are reference points against which actual performance is measured.
    - Derived from planning; should be stated in definite, measurable terms.
    - *Examples*: cost per unit, sales volume, production units per hour, customer satisfaction score, defect rate.
2. *Measuring Actual Performance*
    - Gather data on individual and group activities.
    - Methods: personal observation, written/oral reports, automated tracking systems.
    - *Example*: A supervisor counts units produced per shift; an online dashboard tracks daily website sales.
3. *Comparing Actual Performance Against Standards*
    - Determine the degree of deviation (if any) between standard and actual.
    - Identify whether the deviation is acceptable or needs attention.
    - *Example*: Standard = 100 units/day; Actual = 85 units/day → deviation of -15% triggers investigation.
4. *Taking Corrective Actions*
    - If significant deviation exists, take remedial measures to bring performance back to standard.
    - Actions may include: changing methods, retraining, adjusting resources, revising the standard (if it was unrealistic), or improving supervision.
    - *Example*: After finding low output, the manager replaces a faulty machine and provides additional training, restoring production to standard.

*Note*: The cycle is continuous. After corrective action, performance is re‑measured, and the loop repeats.

## **3. Types of Control Systems**

*Classified by the timing of intervention relative to the work activity.*

**A. Feed‑forward Control (Preliminary / Preventive)**

- Focus: *Before* the activity begins.
- Aims to identify and prevent deviations *in advance*.
- Targets inputs: human, material, financial resources.
- *Example*: A company uses skill tests and background checks when hiring to ensure new employees can meet performance standards. A restaurant pre‑inspects vegetable quality before cooking.

**B. Concurrent Control (Steering / Real‑time)**

- Focus: *During* the activity.
- Monitors ongoing operations to ensure they conform to standards.
- Relies on performance rules, direct supervision, and real‑time measurements.
- *Example*: A manufacturing line has sensors that measure product dimensions as items are made; an operator adjusts the machine immediately if a drift is detected. A chef tastes the sauce while cooking.

**C. Feedback Control (Post‑action / Output)**

- Focus: *After* the activity is completed.
- Reviews final results to see if standards were met; provides information for future planning.
- *Example*: Monthly profit analysis: if target was +12% profit but only +3% is achieved after three months, management investigates causes and adjusts future plans. A final exam score used to decide whether a student passes.

*Analogy*: Feed‑forward = checking the car's oil and fuel before a trip; Concurrent = watching the speedometer while driving; Feedback = checking the trip distance and fuel economy after arriving.

## **4. Characteristics of an Effective Control System**

1. *Integrated with Planning* → Controls must be embedded in the planning process. Anticipated problems should have corresponding control measures.
    
    *Example*: A budget (plan) has monthly spending limits (control).
    
2. *Suitable* → The control system must fit the organization's needs, size, culture, and activity. Over‑control wastes resources; under‑control invites chaos.
3. *Accurate* → Information provided must be reliable and precise. Inaccurate data leads to wrong decisions.
    
    *Example*: A faulty sales report showing high figures when actual sales are low will prevent needed corrective action.
    
4. *Timely* → Information must reach the decision‑maker quickly enough to allow action. A report about last month's defect rate arriving six months late is useless.
5. *Flexible* → The system should adapt to changed conditions, new strategies, or unexpected events.
6. *Economical* → The cost of control should not exceed its benefits. A complex control system for a tiny task wastes resources.
7. *Simple and Understandable* → Users must comprehend the control measures and data. Complicated systems create confusion.
8. *Focus on Strategic Points* → Controls should concentrate on key performance areas (critical success factors) rather than every minute detail.

## **5. Quality Control Systems – Concept of Quality**

- *Quality* → The totality of features and characteristics of a product or service that bear on its ability to satisfy stated or implied needs.
- In modern management, quality is defined by the *customer*: fitness for use, conformance to requirements, and exceeding expectations.
- *Quality Control* → The operational techniques and activities used to fulfill requirements for quality. It involves monitoring processes, inspecting outputs, and eliminating causes of unsatisfactory performance.

*Evolution*: From merely inspecting finished products (sorting good from bad) to building quality into every process (prevention over detection).

## **6. Total Quality Management (TQM) – Concept and Tools**

### **6.1 Concept of TQM**

- *TQM* → A comprehensive, organization‑wide philosophy of continuously improving the quality of products, services, and processes by focusing on customer satisfaction and involving all employees.
- *Core principles*:
    - *Customer focus*: The customer defines quality.
    - *Continuous improvement (Kaizen)*: Never‑ending incremental improvements in every area.
    - *Employee involvement*: All levels participate in problem‑solving and quality initiatives.
    - *Process‑centred*: Quality is achieved by improving processes, not just inspecting outputs.
    - *Fact‑based decision making*: Data and statistical methods guide decisions.

### **6.2 Common TQM Tools**

*These are the "7 Basic Tools of Quality" often associated with TQM.*

1. *Check Sheets* → Structured forms for collecting and organizing data in real time.
    
    *Example*: A daily tally of defect types on a production line.
    
2. *Flowcharts / Process Maps* → Visual representation of a process, showing steps and decision points. Helps identify bottlenecks and waste.
3. *Cause‑and‑Effect Diagram (Ishikawa / Fishbone)* → Identifies root causes of a problem by categorizing potential factors (e.g., Man, Machine, Method, Material, Measurement, Environment).
    
    *Example*: Brainstorming why late deliveries occur, mapping causes on the fishbone.
    
4. *Pareto Chart* → A bar graph ordered by frequency; based on the 80/20 rule (80% of problems come from 20% of causes).
    
    *Example*: 80% of customer complaints stem from just two defect types; prioritize fixing those.
    
5. *Histogram* → A bar chart showing the frequency distribution of a dataset. Reveals variation patterns.
    
    *Example*: Distribution of delivery times; is the process centred on the target?
    
6. *Scatter Diagram* → Plots two variables to see if they are correlated.
    
    *Example*: Plotting training hours vs. product defects to see if more training reduces defects.
    
7. *Control Charts* → A run chart with upper and lower control limits; monitors process stability over time and distinguishes between common cause and special cause variation.
    
    *Example*: Monitoring the diameter of machined parts; a point outside the limit signals a process needing adjustment.

## **7. Deming Management – Principles and Techniques**

*W. Edwards Deming* was a pivotal figure in quality management, especially known for his work in Japan post‑WWII and his 14 Points for Management. His philosophy emphasizes profound knowledge, statistical thinking, and transformation of management.

### **7.1 Deming's 14 Principles (Summarized)**

1. *Create constancy of purpose* → Aim for long‑term improvement, not short‑term profit.
2. *Adopt the new philosophy* → Refuse to accept common levels of delay, error, and defective work.
3. *Cease dependence on mass inspection* → Build quality into the process; inspection is too late and costly.
4. *End the practice of awarding business on price tag alone* → Build long‑term relationships with fewer, quality‑focused suppliers.
5. *Improve constantly and forever* → Continuously improve the system of production and service.
6. *Institute training on the job* → All employees, including management, must be properly trained.
7. *Institute leadership* → Supervision should help people do a better job, not just judge them.
8. *Drive out fear* → Create a safe environment where employees can ask questions and report problems.
9. *Break down barriers between departments* → Teamwork across functions (R&D, design, sales, production).
10. *Eliminate slogans and exhortations* → Remove arbitrary targets and posters asking for zero defects without providing methods.
11. *Eliminate numerical quotas* → Quotas focus on quantity over quality; replace with intelligent leadership.
12. *Remove barriers to pride of workmanship* → Allow people to feel pride in their work; remove annual rating systems that demotivate.
13. *Institute a vigorous program of education and self‑improvement* → Continuous learning for everyone.
14. *Take action to accomplish the transformation* → Top management must lead the change.

### **7.2 Deming's Key Techniques / Philosophy**

- *PDCA Cycle (Plan‑Do‑Check‑Act)*: Also called the Deming Cycle or Shewhart Cycle.
    - *Plan*: Identify a problem, define a hypothesis, design an experiment.
    - *Do*: Implement the change on a small scale.
    - *Check*: Evaluate the results, analyze data.
    - *Act*: If successful, standardize the change; if not, repeat the cycle.
        
        *Example*: A restaurant wants to reduce customer wait time. Plan: test a new table layout. Do: try it for one week. Check: measure average wait time vs. before. Act: if reduced, implement permanently; if not, revise the layout and try again.
        
- *Profound Knowledge System*: Deming's holistic framework comprising appreciation for a system, knowledge of variation, theory of knowledge, and psychology. It emphasizes that management must understand the interdependence of all parts and use data, not gut‑feeling, to drive decisions.
- *Focus on Reducing Variation*: Deming highlighted that uncontrolled variation is the enemy of quality. Statistical methods (control charts) are essential to identify special causes vs. common causes of variation, so management can act appropriately (fix the system, not blame people).
