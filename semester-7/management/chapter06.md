## **1. Concept and Principles of Organizing**

### **1.1 Concept of Organizing**

- *Organizing* → Designing and assigning jobs, grouping tasks, delegating authority, and establishing relationships so that people can work together effectively.
- *Allen*: "The process of identifying and grouping the work to be performed, defining and delegating responsibility and authority, and establishing a pattern of relationship to enable people to work most effectively together in accomplishing objectives."
- *Organizing = Structuring* relationships among people, jobs, and the organisation.

*Understanding* → If planning is drawing the map, organising is building the vehicle and assigning seats.

---

### **1.2 Principles of Organizing**

*Guidelines to create a sound organisation structure.*

- *Objective* → Structure must reflect clearly defined goals for the whole organisation, each department, and each position.
- *Specialization (Division of Labour)* → Break down large tasks into smaller, specialised jobs. Each person concentrates on what they do best.
    
    *Example*: In a restaurant, separate chefs for grilling, salads, and desserts.
    
- *Span of Control* → Limit the number of subordinates a manager can effectively supervise. A smaller span helps maintain efficiency.
- *Scalar Chain* → A clear, unbroken chain of command from top to bottom. Keeps communication and decision‑making orderly.
- *Unity of Command* → Each employee receives orders from *one* superior only. Prevents confusion and conflict.
    
    *Example*: A salesperson reports to only one sales manager.
    
- *Delegation of Authority* → Authority must be delegated sufficiently so that every manager can accomplish assigned tasks.
- *Responsibility* → Managers are responsible for their own actions and those of their subordinates.
- *Efficiency* → The structure should achieve goals at the lowest possible cost with optimum use of resources.
- *Simplicity* → Keep the structure as simple as possible to avoid communication and coordination problems.
- *Flexibility* → The structure should adapt to changes in the external environment.
- *Balance* → Reasonable balance in the size of departments and between centralisation and decentralisation.
- *Unity of Direction* → One objective and one plan for each group of activities with the same purpose.

## **2. Approaches to Organizing**

### **2.1 Classical Approach**

- *Key thinkers*: Taylor, Fayol, Weber.
- Believes in *universal principles* and "one best way" to organise.
- *Taylor*: Scientific job design; separate planning (management) from doing (workers); incentive pay.
- *Fayol*: 14 principles; unity of command, scalar chain, authority–responsibility.
- *Weber's Bureaucracy*: hierarchy, rules & regulations, impersonality, technical qualifications, seniority‑based promotion.
- *Mechanistic view*; treats organisation like a machine.

### **2.2 Behavioural Approach**

- Focuses on *people* as the central element of organised activity.
- Success depends on understanding workers' backgrounds, needs, perceptions, and aspirations.
- Concentrates on social aspects of the job (not just physical).
    
    *Example*: Designing teams to satisfy social needs, not just technical efficiency.
    

### **2.3 Contingency Approach**

- Rejects "one best way". The right structure depends on the *situation*.
- *Contingency variables*:
    - *Technology* → influences coordination needs (routine vs. non‑routine).
    - *Environment* → stable environments suit mechanistic; dynamic environments need organic.
    - *Size* → larger organisations tend to have more complex structures.
    - *Strategic Choice* → structure follows strategy; decide what to do before designing the structure.

## **3. Process of Structuring an Organization**

*Five sequential steps to build an organisation's framework.*

1. *Study of Jobs* → Identify the work and its elements required to achieve objectives.
2. *Grouping of Jobs (Departmentalization)* → Cluster similar or related jobs into departments based on function, product, territory, etc.
3. *Division of Labour* → Assign specific tasks to individuals based on their skills and training.
4. *Deciding on Organisation Design* → Choose between *mechanistic* (rigid, hierarchical, rule‑based) and *organic* (flexible, participative, adaptive) structures based on contingency factors.
5. *Coordination of Activities* → Integrate efforts across departments through leadership and communication to ensure all parts work as a whole.

---

## **4. Departmentalization: Meaning and Types**

- *Departmentalization* → The process of grouping jobs into manageable units (departments) based on a logical arrangement.
- *Shrlekar*: "The process of analysing, dividing, and arranging work or activities into manageable portions for individuals, sections, and departments."

**Types of Departmentalization:**

**A. By Function**

- Groups jobs by business functions: production, marketing, finance, HR.
- *Example*: All accounting staff placed in the Finance Department.
- *Advantages*: specialisation, direct supervision, clear career paths.
- *Disadvantages*: narrow departmental focus, less concern for overall goals.

**B. By Product**

- All activities related to one product or product line are grouped.
- *Example*: A large electronics firm with separate divisions for mobiles, laptops, and appliances.
- *Advantages*: coordination within product, quick decisions, easy performance evaluation.
- *Disadvantages*: resource duplication, reduced cooperation across product divisions.

**C. By Customer/Client**

- Organised around specific customer types to serve their unique needs.
- *Example*: A bank's loan department split into sections for traders, industrialists, and farmers.
- *Advantages*: customer focus, easy to measure performance, promotes general management skills.
- *Disadvantages*: resource duplication, cross‑group cooperation may suffer.

**D. By Territory (Geographic)**

- Groups activities by region, zone, or branch.
- *Example*: Insurance company with Eastern, Central, and Western regional offices.
- *Advantages*: locally responsive, deep regional knowledge, clear regional accountability.
- *Disadvantages*: cross‑regional coordination difficult, duplication of functions.

**E. By Process**

- Suitable for manufacturing where distinct processes exist.
- *Example*: Textile mill with departments for spinning, weaving, dyeing, and finishing.
- *Advantages*: clear authority, efficient resource use.
- *Disadvantages*: mainly for manufacturing, risk of coordination gaps.

**F. By Time**

- For organisations operating 24 hours; departments based on shifts.
- *Example*: Hospital with day, evening, and night shifts.
- *Advantages*: optimum resource use, flexible staffing.
- *Disadvantages*: scheduling complexity, overtime costs, coordination across shifts.

## **5. Delegation of Authority**

- *Delegation* → The process by which a manager assigns a portion of his/her workload and the necessary authority to a subordinate.
- *Griffin*: "The process by which a manager assigns a portion of his or her total workload to others."

**Features:**

- *Assigning Responsibility* → Creates an obligation to perform the given duty.
- *Granting Authority* → Transfers the right to make decisions and use resources.
- *Creating Accountability* → The subordinate becomes answerable for performance; accountability flows upward.

**Advantages:**

- Reduces top management's workload; frees them for strategic issues.
- Develops subordinates' decision‑making skills.
- Leads to better, faster decisions (decision‑maker is close to the action).
- Increases employee motivation and acceptance of accountability.

**Barriers to Effective Delegation:**

- *Manager's reluctance*: lack of trust, fear of competition from skilled subordinates.
- *Subordinates' unwillingness*: no extra reward for additional responsibility.
- *Organisational climate*: rigid structure, unclear authority lines.

*Key principle*: Authority and responsibility must be in parity (equal). Responsibility cannot be delegated; the delegator remains ultimately accountable.

## **6. Centralization and Decentralization**

- *Centralization* → Systematic retention of power and decision‑making authority in the hands of top‑level managers.
    
    *Griffin*: "The process of systematically retaining power in the hands of higher‑level managers."
    
- *Decentralization* → Systematic dispersal of decision‑making authority to the lowest levels where work is performed.
    
    *Koontz & Weihrich*: "The tendency to disperse decision‑making authority in an organised structure."
    

**Advantages of Centralization:**

- Uniformity of policies and practices.
- Minimised duplication of functions.
- Strong coordinated top‑management team.
- Full utilisation of central office facilities.

**Disadvantages of Centralization:**

- Work overload on top management.
- Less development opportunity for middle/lower managers.
- Close supervision may demotivate skilled employees.

**Advantages of Decentralization:**

- Motivates employees through participation.
- Develops managerial skills at all levels.
- Quicker response to local conditions.
- Reduces burden on top management.

**Disadvantages of Decentralization:**

- Difficult to maintain uniform policies.
- Risk of misuse of authority if managers are unskilled.
- Higher cost for training and development.
- Requires strong planning and control systems.

*Note*: Decentralization is an extension of delegation. When delegation is applied organisation‑wide and systematically, it results in decentralization.

## **7. Organic and Mechanistic Views of Organization**

*Two contrasting designs for structuring organisations.*

| **Aspect** | **Mechanistic View** | **Organic View** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Structure* | Rigid, tall hierarchy | Flexible, flat, network‑like |
| *Rules & Procedures* | Many formal rules, standardised | Few rules, adaptable |
| *Decision‑making* | Centralised at top | Decentralised, participative |
| *Communication* | Vertical, top‑down | Lateral, free‑flowing |
| *Environment* | Stable, predictable | Dynamic, uncertain |
| *Job Design* | Narrow, specialised | Broad, enriched |
| *Example* | Classic assembly‑line factory | Tech startup, R&D lab |

*Choosing between them*: The contingency approach determines which is appropriate. Mechanistic suits routine tasks and stable environments; organic suits innovation, rapid change, and complex environments.

## **8. Types of Modern Organizational Structures**

*The syllabus highlights three modern forms: matrix, team, and network.*

### **8.1 Matrix Structure**

- *Also called*: project organization, grid, multiple command system.
- *Design*: Combines functional and project‑based departments. Employees report to *two bosses* — a functional manager and a project manager.
- *Example*: An engineer works daily under the engineering head (functional) but is assigned to a specific new‑product project and reports to the project manager for that work.
- *Advantages*: efficient resource use, flexibility, improved decision quality, personal development.
- *Disadvantages*: slower decisions, power struggles, ambiguous accountability, role conflict.

### **8.2 Team Structure**

- *Concept*: The entire organisation or large parts of it are built around self‑managing teams rather than rigid departments.
- Team members decide on inputs, schedules, task allocation, and even performance evaluation.
- *Advantages*: enhances performance, reduces stress, promotes creativity and innovation.
- *Example*: A software firm where cross‑functional teams (designers, developers, testers) handle complete products from start to finish.

### **8.3 Network (Virtual) Structure**

- *Also called*: virtual organization.
- *Design*: The organisation creates a network of external partners and contractors to perform most core functions — manufacturing, distribution, accounting, HR — keeping only the most essential strategic activities in‑house.
- *Example*: A fashion brand that designs clothes but outsources manufacturing to factories, logistics to shipping firms, and sales to online retail partners.
- *Advantages*: high flexibility, low overhead, access to specialised expertise.
- *Disadvantages*: less direct control, dependency on partners, possible coordination challenges.
