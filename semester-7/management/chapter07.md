## **1. Concept and Functions of Leadership**

### **1.1 Concept of Leadership**

- *Leadership* → The ability to build confidence and zeal among people and to create an urge in them to be led.
- *Mary Parker Follett*: "The leader stimulates what is best in us … gives form to the uncoarctate energy in every man."
- *Koontz & O'Donnell*: "The ability of a manager to induce subordinates to work with confidence and zeal."
- *Key idea*: Leadership is about *influence*, not coercion. Followers follow willingly.

**Nature / Characteristics of Leadership:**

- Personal quality.
- Exists only with followers; willingness of people to follow makes a leader.
- Process of influence over behaviour, attitude, and beliefs.
- Aims at realisation of common goals.
- Involves readiness to accept complete responsibility.
- Leadership styles change with circumstances.
- It is not "bossism" and is distinct from mere management.

*Understanding* → A manager is a position; a leader earns voluntary followers. Leadership can exist at any level.

---

### **1.2 Functions of a Leader**

1. *Setting Goals* → Lay out creative goals and policies; persuade subordinates to work with zeal.
2. *Organizing* → Shape the organization scientifically; assign roles matching individual abilities.
3. *Initiating Action* → Take initiative; float new ideas and make original decisions rather than depending on others.
4. *Co‑ordination* → Reconcile interests of individuals with organizational goals; secure voluntary co‑operation.
5. *Direction and Motivation* → Guide and motivate people to do their best; build confidence.
6. *Link between Management and Workers* → Interpret management policies to workers and represent workers' interests before management.

## **2. Leadership Styles**

*Three classic styles based on decision‑making authority.*

| **Style** | **Description** | **Example** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Autocratic* | Leader makes all decisions; imposes them and expects obedience. No consultation. | Army commander giving an order; a CEO dictating a merger decision without input. |
| *Democratic* | Leader involves employees in decision‑making, delegates authority, encourages participation. | A project manager holding a vote on the team's sprint priorities. |
| *Laissez‑faire (Free‑rein)* | Leader gives group complete freedom; members decide methods and goals. | A university research group where the professor provides resources but lets researchers set their own direction. |

*The choice of style depends on situation, task urgency, and team competence.*

## **3. Approaches to Leadership**

### **3.1 Trait Theory**

- Assumes *leaders possess certain innate traits* that non‑leaders lack.
- *Common traits identified*: Drive, desire to lead, honesty & integrity, self‑confidence, intelligence, job‑relevant knowledge, extraversion (outgoingness, high energy).
- *Limitation*: No universal set of traits predicts leadership success in all situations.

### **3.2 Behavioral Theories**

- Focus on *what effective leaders do*, not who they are. Leadership can be learned.
- *Four leadership behaviors* (from path‑goal theory, often used):
    - *Directive leadership* → Gives clear instructions, expectations, schedules.
    - *Supportive leadership* → Shows concern for subordinate needs and well‑being.
    - *Participative leadership* → Consults subordinates and uses their suggestions before deciding.
    - *Achievement‑oriented leadership* → Sets challenging goals and expects high performance.
- *Example*: A manager might use directive style with new hires, then shift to participative as they gain experience.

### **3.3 Situational Theory**

- *No single best style*; effective leadership depends on the situation.
- Particularly, the style should match the *follower's readiness* (ability and willingness to do a task).
- *If followers are unable and unwilling* → Use directive style.
    
    *If followers are able and willing* → Use delegating / laissez‑faire style.
    
- *Core concept*: Leaders must diagnose the situation and adapt their style accordingly.

## **4. Group Formation**

- *Group* → Two or more individuals, interacting and interdependent, who come together to achieve particular objectives.

**Stages of Group Development (Tuckman's Model):**

1. *Forming* → Members are unsure of purpose; they look to the leader for direction. Politeness, anxiety, curiosity.
    
    *Example*: First day of a new project team; everyone is getting to know each other.
    
2. *Storming* → Conflict and friction arise as members push boundaries and express different ideas.
    
    *Example*: Team argues over the best technical approach; personality clashes surface.
    
3. *Norming* → Differences are resolved; cohesion develops; members appreciate each other's strengths and respect the leader.
    
    *Example*: Team agrees on communication protocols and role divisions.
    
4. *Performing* → The team works as a unit, efficiently achieving goals. High trust, clear processes.
    
    *Example*: A surgical team working seamlessly during a complex operation.
    
5. *Adjourning (Mourning)* → Task is completed; group disbands. Mixed feelings of achievement and loss.
    
    *Example*: Project end party; team members redeployed to other projects.

## **5. Types and Characteristics of Groups**

### **5.1 Types of Groups**

- *Formal Groups* → Created by the organisation to coordinate work activities; have defined roles.
    
    *Example*: A finance department, a quality control committee.
    
- *Informal Groups* → Formed spontaneously based on personal relationships and shared interests; satisfy psychological and social needs.
    
    *Example*: Colleagues who lunch together daily and support each other.
    
- *Managed Groups* → Members share a similar work type but may not interact much.
    
    *Example*: All the science teachers in a school, grouped under a "Science Department" label.
    
- *Process Groups* → Members follow a fixed sequence of steps together.
    
    *Example*: An assembly‑line team where each worker adds a component in a set order.
    
- *Goal Groups* → Formed to achieve a specific shared objective.
    
    *Example*: A taskforce created to reduce costs by 10% in six months.
    
- *Learning Groups* → Formed to increase knowledge or skills.
    
    *Example*: A book club at work, a study circle for a professional certification.
    
- *Problem‑Solving Groups* → Come together to address a specific issue.
    
    *Example*: A team investigating a sudden drop in customer satisfaction.
    
- *Interest Groups* → Members affiliate to achieve a personal or shared objective, may cross formal lines.
    
    *Example*: An employee union, a group advocating for a company daycare facility.
    
- *Task / Functional Groups* → Formed around a task or function and remain for a long period.
    
    *Example*: The permanent IT support team.
    
- *Interacting Groups* → Members' performance depends on the completion of each other's assigned tasks (sequential interdependence).
    
    *Example*: A car production line where engine installation must happen before the body is fitted.
    

### **5.2 Characteristics of Groups**

- *Interaction*: members communicate and influence one another.
- *Interdependence*: rely on each other's efforts.
- *Common objective(s)*.
- *Shared norms and roles*.
- *Cohesiveness*: a sense of belonging.

## **6. Conflict: Meaning and Types**

- *Conflict* → A serious disagreement and argument about something important. In the workplace, it arises from unmet expectations, personality clashes, or value differences.

### **6.1 Types of Conflict**

| **Type** | **Source** | **Example** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *Task Conflict* | Disputes about work content, resource division, procedures, or interpretation of facts. | Two managers argue over how to allocate the annual budget between marketing and R&D. |
| *Relationship Conflict* | Differences in personality, style, taste; often emotional friction. | A detail‑oriented accountant finds a creative designer's casual approach frustrating; they clash personally. |
| *Value Conflict* | Fundamental differences in deeply held beliefs, ethics, politics, or norms. | A team debates whether to take on a client with ties to a corrupt government; one member finds it morally unacceptable. |

## **7. Managing Conflicts in Organizations**

*A structured mediation framework with seven key steps.*

1. *Bring Both Parties Together*
    - Choose a neutral, private meeting place.
    - Make each side feel safe to express themselves honestly.
2. *Lay Out the Ground Rules*
    - Mediator's role: guide toward a mutually beneficial outcome, not assign blame.
    - Focus on the shared problem; no aggressive behaviour or language.
    - Maintain respect and dignity.
3. *Find the Root Cause of the Conflict*
    - Move beyond symptoms; identify the real underlying problem.
        
        *Example*: A fight over overtime pay may actually stem from unclear communication about expectations.
        
4. *Actively Listen as Each Side Has Their Say*
    - Give equal time and space; avoid finger‑pointing.
    - No single person dominates the conversation.
5. *Establish a Desired Outcome*
    - Ask each party what they hope to achieve.
    - Find a shared goal (e.g., a calmer, more harmonious work environment).
6. *Get Participants to Suggest Potential Solutions*
    - Brainstorm courses of action each side can live with.
    - Discard unworkable options jointly.
7. *Implementation*
    - Have each participant acknowledge their responsibilities.
    - Document specific actions, obligations, and timelines if needed.
        
        *Bottom line*: Ownership of the solution increases the chance of lasting resolution.
        

*Additional crisis note*: For deeper, more complex disputes, mediation by a neutral third party is often the most effective method.
