## **1. Concept of Business Environment**

- *Business Environment* → The sum total of all forces surrounding and influencing the life and development of an organization.

> *Gareth*: "The set of forces surrounding an organization that have the potential to affect the way it operates and its access to scarce resources."
> 
- *Key Idea*: The environment creates conditions that influence a firm's capacity to compete. Survival = ability to *adapt* to changing environmental forces.

---

## **2. Types of Business Environment**

The environment is divided into two broad categories: **Internal** and **External**.

---

### **2.1 Internal Environment**

*Forces and conditions within the organisation that directly influence behaviour.*

- *Employees* → Main asset; their cooperation and productivity determine goal achievement.
    
    [Example: Skilled, motivated workers lead to higher quality output.]
    
- *Shareholders & Board of Directors* → Owners (shareholders) elect directors who set strategy and oversee management.
    
    [Example: The board approves a five-year expansion plan and evaluates the CEO's performance.]
    
- *Organizational Culture* → Shared values, beliefs, and norms that bind employees and guide decision‑making.
    
    [Example: A culture of innovation encourages employees to experiment without fear of failure.]
    
- *Labour Unions* → Represent workers' interests; good union‑management relations prevent disruptions.
    
    [Example: Regular dialogue avoids strikes and maintains smooth operations.]
    

---

### **2.2 External Environment**

*Forces outside the organisation that affect its operations. Two sub‑categories:*

- *General Environment* (Macro) — Affects all firms indirectly.
- *Task (Specific) Environment* — Directly impacts the day‑to‑day business.

---

## **3. Components of the External Environment**

### **3.1 General Environment (PEST Analysis Framework)**

**A. Economic Environment**

- Economic system, policies, and conditions that shape the market.
- *Components*: Economic system, monetary/fiscal policies, business cycle, capital market.
- *Factors*: National income, savings, investment, interest rates, inflation, employment, consumption patterns.
- *Manager's Task*: Forecast economic changes and adapt.
    
    [Example: A rise in interest rates increases loan costs → firm may postpone expansion; falling unemployment signals rising consumer spending, so a retailer stocks more inventory.]
    

**B. Socio‑Cultural Environment**

- Values, beliefs, lifestyles, family systems, religion, education, language, class system — all influence consumer behaviour and workforce attitudes.
    
    [Example: A society that values health and fitness creates demand for organic food and gym services; a hierarchical class system may affect team dynamics in the workplace.]
    

**C. Political Environment**

- Government institutions, political parties, laws, and stability.
- Why important:
    - Imposes legal constraints (licenses, taxes, labour laws).
    - Creates pro‑ or anti‑business climate.
    - Provides (or denies) stability for long‑term planning.
        
        [Example: A government announcing corporate tax cuts signals a pro‑business stance; frequent policy flip‑flops deter foreign investment.]
        

**D. Technological Environment**

- Practical application of scientific knowledge; includes automation, IT, communication, robotics.
- Brings both opportunities (new products, efficiency) and threats (obsolescence, competition).
    
    [Example: E‑commerce technology enables a small handicraft seller to reach global customers, but also exposes it to international competitors. A factory upgrading to automation reduces cost but displaces manual workers.]
    

---

### **3.2 Task (Specific) Environment**

*Direct, immediate external forces.*

- *Customers* → Individuals, families, businesses who buy products/services. Provide revenue *and* feedback.
    
    [Example: A restaurant adjusts its menu based on customer reviews and preferences.]
    
- *Suppliers* → Provide resources (raw materials, capital, equipment). Quality and price of inputs affect final output.
    
    [Example: A smartphone maker depends on reliable chip suppliers; a delay in supply halts production.]
    
- *Government* (as specific actor) → Regulates, inspects, and enforces rules that directly impact business practices.
    
    [Example: Food safety authority inspects a restaurant kitchen; a violation can lead to closure.]
    
- *Competitors* → Rival firms competing for the same resources and customers. Must analyse and differentiate.
    
    [Example: Two airlines on the same route compete on price, schedule, and service quality.]
    
- *Media* → Watches and reports on business actions of public interest. Can shape reputation.
    
    [Example: A news report on a company's environmental violation can trigger public outrage and boycott.]
    
- *Financial Institutions* → Banks, insurance companies, merchant banks that supply funds. Loan terms affect viability.
    
    [Example: A start‑up depends on a bank loan; a high‑interest condition reduces profit margins.]
    
- *Special Interest Groups* → Environmentalists, consumer advocates, unions, professional bodies that pressurise firms on specific issues (quality, waste, pricing).
    
    [Example: A consumer rights group forces a telecom company to improve network quality after a campaign.]

## **4. Social Responsibility of Business**

### **4.1 Concept**

*Social Responsibility* → The obligation of a business to act in ways that benefit society, beyond just pursuing profits.

*Core idea*: Business does not operate in a vacuum; it must consider the impact of its actions on all stakeholders.

### **4.2 Approaches to Social Responsibility**

*Two classical extremes and a modern middle path:*

- *Classical (Friedman) View* → "The only social responsibility of business is to increase its profits."
    - Maximise shareholder wealth while obeying the law.
    - [Example: A firm focuses solely on cost‑cutting and revenue, ignoring community protests about pollution, as long as it complies with minimum legal standards.]
- *Socio‑Economic (Stakeholder) View* → Business is part of society and must serve broader social needs.
    - Protect and improve welfare of society, even beyond legal requirements.
    - [Example: A company voluntarily reduces carbon emissions beyond what the law requires, invests in employee education, and supports local infrastructure.]
- *Continuum of Responsibility*:
    - *Social Obligation* – meet only legal and economic minimums (compliance).
    - *Social Responsiveness* – respond to current societal demands and preferences.
    - *Social Responsibility* (proactive) – anticipate and act on future social needs; go beyond expectations.
        
        [Example: A bank that offers low‑interest loans to rural women not because of a law but because it believes in financial inclusion is showing proactive social responsibility.]
        

---

## **5. Areas of Social Responsibility**

*Key domains where businesses should act responsibly:*

- *Organisational Stakeholders*
    - Employees: fair wages, safe workplace, no discrimination, training.
    - Shareholders: transparency, return on investment.
    - Suppliers/creditors: fair dealing, timely payment.
    - Government: paying taxes, obeying regulations.
    - Local community: generating employment, respecting local culture.
        
        [Example: A factory ensures equal pay for equal work and provides health insurance to workers.]
        
- *The Natural Environment*
    - Minimise pollution (air, water, soil), reduce resource depletion, adopt sustainable practices.
    - [Example: A paper manufacturer invests in reforestation and wastewater treatment to neutralise its environmental footprint.]
- *General Social Welfare*
    - Charitable contributions, building schools/hospitals, supporting arts and culture, disaster relief.
    - [Example: A corporate hospital runs free health camps in remote villages, not as marketing but as part of its mission.]

## **6. Business Ethics**

### **6.1 Meaning**

- *Business Ethics* → Personal beliefs about whether a behaviour, action, or decision is right or wrong in a business context.

> *Griffin*: "Ethics is an individual's personal belief about whether a behavior, action or decision is right and wrong."
> 
- It is about moral conduct, duty, and judgment applied in managerial decisions.

*Sources of Management Ethics:*

- *Social Ethics* → values from society's laws, customs, norms.
    
    [Example: Societal norm against child labour → company refuses to hire underage workers even where legal loopholes exist.]
    
- *Professional Ethics* → standards set by professional bodies.
    
    [Example: Chartered accountants follow a code of integrity; HR managers maintain confidentiality.]
    
- *Individual Ethics* → personal values from family, upbringing, experiences.
    
    [Example: A manager with strong personal honesty refuses a bribe even if it means losing a contract.]
    

### **6.2 Significance of Business Ethics**

- *Enhances reputation and goodwill* → Ethical behaviour builds trust, a valuable intangible asset.
    
    [Example: A brand known for fair trade practices attracts loyal customers.]
    
- *Avoids criticism and penalty* → Unethical acts lead to boycotts, legal fines, and loss of credibility.
    
    [Example: A food company caught using harmful additives faces product bans and lawsuits.]
    
- *Promotes economic and social development* → Fair competition, prudent resource use, and ethical practices create a healthy business climate that benefits society at large.
- *Ensures transparency and credibility* → Ethical businesses keep promises, report honestly, and gain long‑term stability.
- *Protects wider society* → Ethical actions safeguard consumer rights, environment, and community interests.
- *Creates a climate of justice, freedom, equity* → Inside the organisation, ethics ensures fair treatment, non‑discrimination, and respect.

## **7. Emerging Business Environment in Nepal**

*Post‑1990s liberalisation and ongoing political transformation shape Nepal's unique environmental context.*

### **7.1 Economic Environment**

- *Mixed economy*: public and private sectors coexist.
- *Slow growth*: GDP growth rate below 3%; per capita income remains low.
- *High inflation* (over 10%) hits low‑income groups.
- *Large trade deficit*: Imports from India and China surge, exports lag due to weak product quality.
- *Globalisation impact*: Small and cottage industries face stiff competition; brain‑drain drains skilled workforce.
- *Positive signs*: Quality management reforms and export‑oriented industries offer hope.
    
    [Example: Pashmina and handicraft producers adopting international quality standards to compete globally.]
    

### **7.2 Political and Legal Environment**

- *Instability*: Frequent government changes, strikes (*bandhs*), and protests disrupt business.
- *Policy inconsistency*: Rules change with new governments, creating uncertainty for investors.
- *Law enforcement gaps*: Violations occur with political backing; labour union issues, human rights concerns, and civic unrest not fully addressed.
- *Constitutional vacuum*: Delay in drafting a new constitution adds to confusion.
    
    [Example: A factory owner cannot plan long‑term expansion because labour laws and tax policies may shift suddenly after the next election.]
    

### **7.3 Socio‑Cultural Environment**

- *Diversity*: Multi‑caste, multi‑religion, varied education levels, urban‑rural divide.
- *Caste‑based discrimination* persists in remote areas; literacy rates lag in villages.
- *Migration*: Youth outflow to Middle Eastern countries for employment alters workforce availability and remittance‑driven consumption patterns.
- *Hierarchy*: Status‑consciousness in society and organisations; favouritism and nepotism are common.
- *Strengths*: Simplicity, hospitality, honesty, and harmony with nature can be leveraged for ethical business models.
    
    [Example: A tourism enterprise highlighting Nepali warmth and eco‑friendly practices attracts international travellers.]
    

### **7.4 Technological Environment**

- *Uneven adoption*: Banking, telecom, and IT sectors use modern technology; agriculture and traditional industries lag.
- *Financial constraints* limit small firms from upgrading.
- *Government apathy* in R&D and tech transfer hampers innovation.
- *Opportunity*: Leapfrogging with mobile banking, renewable energy, and IT services.
    
    [Example: Digital payment platforms like eSewa and FonePay boom despite infrastructure challenges; a small farmer uses a mobile app to check market prices.]
    

*Overall Outlook*: Nepal's business environment is dynamic, filled with challenges of instability and underdevelopment, yet pockets of innovation and resilience show a path forward. Managers must navigate political risk, leverage socio‑cultural strengths, invest in appropriate technology, and adopt ethical, globally competitive practices.
