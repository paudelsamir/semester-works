## **1. Planning: Concept, Types, and Hierarchy**

### **1.1 Concept of Planning**

- *Planning* → The *primary* management function; deciding in advance *what, when, where, why, how* and *who*.
- *Koontz & O'Donnell*: "An intellectual process, the conscious determination of a course of action, the basing of decisions on purpose, facts and considered estimates."
- *Planning =* a documented statement of goals + action‑oriented strategies + resource assignment.

*Understanding* → Planning is like drawing a map before a journey. Without it, you may move fast but in the wrong direction.

---

### **1.2 Types of Planning**

**A. By Managerial Hierarchy (Scope)**

- *Strategic Plan (Grand Plan)* → Long‑term, organisation‑wide, externally oriented.
    - Prepared by *top management*.
    - Addresses mission, purpose, and overall direction.
    - *Example*: A car manufacturer decides to become the market leader in electric vehicles by 2030.
- *Tactical Plan* → Translates strategic goals into specific functional targets.
    - Prepared by *middle‑level managers* (department heads).
    - Focuses on major actions a unit must take.
    - *Example*: The marketing department's plan to launch an EV awareness campaign and open 20 new dealerships in two years.
- *Operational Plan* → Day‑to‑day procedures and routines.
    - Prepared by *frontline managers/supervisors*.
    - Converts tactical objectives into individual assignments.
    - *Example*: A daily production schedule: "Assemble 50 battery packs per shift."

**B. Contingency Plan**

- *Plan B* — an alternative course of action if the original plan fails or an unexpected event occurs.
- Components: Business Impact Analysis → Incident Response → Disaster Recovery → Business Continuity.

**C. By Time Frame**

- Long‑range (5+ years), Medium‑range (2–4 years), Short‑range (≤1 year).

**D. By Frequency of Use**

- *Standing Plans*: repeated use — policies, procedures, rules.
- *Single‑use Plans*: one‑time — programmes, projects, budgets.

---

### **1.3 Hierarchy of Plans (Top to Bottom)**

*This hierarchy moves from broad inspiration to specific action.*

- *Vision* → Non‑specific, directional, motivational. Has emotional appeal.
    
    *Example*: "A world where every Nepali has access to affordable healthcare."
    
- *Mission* → Purpose and philosophy; the reason the organisation exists (5–10 years).
    
    *Example*: "To provide quality, low‑cost medicines through an innovative supply chain."
    
- *Goals* → Targeted ends for the next 1–2 years, stating how the mission will be pursued.
    
    *Example*: "Expand distribution to all 77 districts."
    
- *Objectives* → Specific, measurable statements (*SMART* – Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Rewarding, Timed).
    
    *Example*: "Open 30 new pharmacy outlets in rural areas by December."
    
- *Strategies* → Courses of action that allocate resources efficiently to achieve objectives.
    
    *Example*: "Partner with local cooperatives for last‑mile delivery."
    

---

### **1.4 Methods of Planning**

- *Top‑Down Planning* (Centralised) → Headquarters develops guidelines and plans → disseminated to lower levels.
    - Fast; relies on top‑level expertise.
    - *Example*: Corporate office sets annual sales targets for all regional branches.
- *Bottom‑Up Planning* (Decentralised) → Lower levels draft plans → sent up for review and approval.
    - Encourages participation and commitment.
    - *Example*: Branch managers propose their own sales forecasts, compiled into the national plan.
- *Management by Objectives (MBO)* → Joint goal‑setting by superiors and subordinates.
    - Focuses on results, self‑control, and coaching.
    - Blends planning and control.
    - *Example*: A supervisor and employee together set quarterly performance targets and review dates.

---

### **1.5 Planning Process (Steps)**

1. *Define Mission & Goals* → Clarify the organisation's purpose and direction.
2. *Analyse Environmental Forces* → Scan internal and external factors.
3. *Identify Opportunities & Threats* → Spot favorable trends and possible dangers.
4. *Analyse Organisational Resources* → Examine strengths (capital, skills, information).
5. *Formulate Plans* → Draft comprehensive, integrated plans covering all levels.
6. *Implement Plans* → Put plans into action; ensure resources, motivate staff.
7. *Evaluate & Control* → Monitor progress, identify deviations, adjust.

*Tip* → Planning is a cycle, not a one‑time event. Feedback loops back into future plans.

---

### **1.6 Importance of Planning**

- *Provides Direction* → Gives a clear future path for all managers.
- *Reduces Uncertainty* → Forecasting helps anticipate and meet future challenges.
- *Improves Competitive Strength* → Enables discovery of new opportunities.
- *Aids Coordination* → Aligns individual efforts toward common goals.
- *Ensures Better Resource Utilisation* → Prevents shortages and reduces waste.
- *Encourages Innovation & Creativity* → Sound planning promotes new thinking.
- *Basis of Control* → Goals act as standards against which performance is measured.

## **2. Strategic Planning**

- *Definition* → Process of determining how to pursue long‑term goals with expected resources.
- *Key Features*:
    - *Master Planning*: corporate‑level plan crafted by the Board and CEO.
    - *Top‑down Approach*: formulated centrally, disseminated for implementation.
    - *Highly Prescribed*: detailed roles and responsibilities for all units.
    - *Detailed Control*: central monitoring to ensure implementation.

---

### **2.1 Environmental Scanning**

- *Concept* → Acquiring information and systematically analysing emerging trends in the environment.
- *Wheeler & Hunger*: "Monitoring, evaluating, and disseminating information from external and internal environments to key people."
- *Essence*: Identify relevant changes → monitor their direction → forecast rate & impact → respond strategically.

**Methods of Environmental Scanning:**

- *Extrapolation Method* → Past data used to project future trends (trend analysis, regression).
    
    *Example*: Using last 5 years' sales growth to predict next year's demand.
    
- *Intuitive Reasoning* → Scanner uses rational intuition and experience.
    
    *Example*: A seasoned manager "senses" a shift in consumer taste before data confirms it.
    
- *Scenario Building* → Hypothetical "what‑if" situations with logical cause‑effect chains.
    
    *Example*: "What if a new competitor enters with 30% lower price? How would we react?"
    
- *Delphi Technique* → Experts individually provide opinions through multiple rounds of questionnaires and feedback.
    
    *Example*: Five economists anonymously forecast next year's inflation rate; results refined over rounds.
    

---

### **2.2 SWOT Analysis**

*A tool to match internal capabilities with external environment.*

- *S – Strengths* (Internal +) → Resources, skills, advantages over competitors.
    
    *Example*: Strong brand image, skilled R&D team, loyal customer base.
    
- *W – Weaknesses* (Internal –) → Limitations that hinder performance.
    
    *Example*: Outdated technology, weak distribution network, high employee turnover.
    
- *O – Opportunities* (External +) → Favourable situations to exploit.
    
    *Example*: Government subsidy on renewable energy, rising demand for organic food.
    
- *T – Threats* (External –) → Unfavourable situations to counter.
    
    *Example*: New aggressive competitor, changing regulatory norms, economic downturn.
    

*SWOT Matrix* → The best strategy maximises Strengths–Opportunities (SO) matches and mitigates Weaknesses–Threats (WT) risks.

---

### **2.3 Formulation of Strategic Plans (Steps)**

1. *Evaluate Current Performance* → Assess profits, ROI, market share vs. mission.
2. *Review Corporate Governance* → Evaluate top management's ability to set vision/mission.
3. *External Environment Analysis* → Scan for opportunities and threats.
4. *Internal Environment Analysis* → Identify core competencies (strengths) and weaknesses.
5. *Analyse Strategic Factors* → Pinpoint critical issues; generate strategic alternatives.
6. *Evaluate & Select the Best Option* → Use SWOT matrix to choose a strategy that fits internal strengths with external opportunities.

---

### **2.4 Implementation of Strategic Plans**

- *Strategy Implementation* → "Working the plan" into action.
- A worthless exercise if not implemented properly.
- Affects all levels and functions; requires resource alignment, motivation, and strong communication.

---

## **3. Quantitative Tools for Planning**

- *PERT (Program Evaluation & Review Technique)* → A network/flow‑chart showing project activities.
    - *Events*: start/end points of activities.
    - *Activities*: time or resources needed between events.
    - *Slack Time*: allowable delay without affecting the project deadline.
    - *Critical Path*: the longest sequence; determines the minimum project duration.
        
        *Example*: Constructing a bridge – identifying that foundation work and steel erection are on the critical path, so any delay there pushes the whole project.
        
- *Break‑Even (BE) Analysis (Cost‑Volume‑Profit)* → Determines the quantity at which total revenue = total cost.
    - Helps in pricing and profit decisions.
        
        *Example*: How many cups of coffee must a café sell to cover rent, wages, and materials before making a profit?
        
- *Linear Programming* → Optimising an objective (max profit or min cost) under limited resources.
    
    *Example*: A furniture factory decides the best combination of tables and chairs to produce within available wood and labour hours to maximise profit.
    
- *Gantt Chart* → Horizontal bar chart with time on X‑axis and activities on Y‑axis.
    - Shows start/end of each task, allows at‑a‑glance progress monitoring.
        
        *Example*: A marketing campaign schedule – bars show when graphic design, printing, and social media ads start and end; delays are instantly visible.

## **4. Decision Making**

### **4.1 Definition and Process**

- *Decision* → A choice made from available alternatives.
- *Decision Making* → Conscious process of selecting a course of action to achieve a goal or solve a problem.

**Decision‑Making Process (6 Steps):**

1. *Identify & Diagnose the Problem* → Accurately recognise deviation.
    
    *Example*: Declining sales – root cause: poor advertising, not product quality.
    
2. *Generate Possible Alternatives* → List all feasible solutions.
3. *Evaluate Alternatives* → Assess each against criteria (feasibility, cost, time, effectiveness).
4. *Select the Best Alternative* → Choose the one that maximises goal achievement.
    
    *Selection reasons*: cheaper, faster, more effective, preferred by staff, higher productivity.
    
5. *Implement the Selected Alternative* → Put it into action with communication and cooperation.
6. *Evaluate & Control* → Monitor results; if problem persists, restart the process.

---

### **4.2 Approaches to Decision Making**

- *Classical Model* (Prescriptive, based on economic rationality)
    - Assumes: clear objectives, full information, all alternatives known, rational evaluation, maximisation of return.
    - Works best under *certainty* or for programmed decisions.
    - *Limitation*: Unrealistic for complex, uncertain problems.
- *Administrative Model* (Herbert Simon)
    - Based on *bounded rationality*: decision makers are limited by beliefs, skills, time, incomplete information.
    - They *satisfice* — select the first satisfactory solution rather than searching for the optimal one.
        
        *Example*: A manager hires the first candidate who meets basic requirements, not necessarily the absolute best possible, because searching exhaustively is impractical.
        

---

### **4.3 Types of Decisions**

**A. By Frequency**

- *Programmed Decisions* → Repetitive, routine; solved by policies, rules, SOPs.
    
    *Example*: Reordering office supplies when stock falls to a preset level.
    
- *Non‑Programmed Decisions* → Novel, unstructured, complex; require judgment and creativity.
    
    *Example*: Deciding to enter an entirely new foreign market.
    

**B. By Nature**

- *Operating Decisions* (Internal) → Day‑to‑day efficiency, production schedule, inventory. Taken by lower management.
- *Strategic Decisions* (External focus) → Goals, diversification, expansion. Taken by top management.
- *Administrative Decisions* → Bridge the two; rules, procedures, reward systems, information flow. Taken by middle management.

---

### **4.4 Decision Making Conditions**

- *Certainty* → All alternatives, conditions, and consequences are known.
    
    *Example*: Buying a printer after comparing exact specs and prices from different vendors.
    
    - Decision is simple and straightforward.
- *Risk* → Alternatives known, but outcomes are probabilistic (less than 100%).
    
    *Example*: Launching a new product with a 70% chance of success based on market research. The manager faces risk but can assign probabilities.
    
- *Uncertainty* → Little or no information about alternatives or future environment.
    
    *Example*: Investing in a politically unstable country where laws may change overnight.
    
    - Manager relies on intuition, judgment, and experience.

---

### **4.5 Decision‑Making Styles (Vroom–Yetton baseline)**

- *Autocratic*
    - *Style I*: Manager decides alone using available information.
    - *Style II*: Manager gathers information from subordinates, then decides alone.
- *Consultative*
    - *Style III*: Manager shares problem individually with relevant subordinates, gets ideas, then decides (may or may not use their input).
    - *Style IV*: Manager shares problem with group collectively, gets ideas, then decides.
- *Group Process*
    - *Style V*: Manager shares problem with group; together they generate, evaluate, and reach consensus. Manager implements the group's supported solution.

*Choosing the style* depends on time, importance of decision, and need for commitment.

## **5. Problem Solving**

### **5.1 Concept**

- *Problem* → Deviation between actual and desired situation.
- *Problem Solving* → Using information to minimise that deviation, selecting alternatives, resolving disputes, and making an optimum choice.

---

### **5.2 Types of Problems**

**A. By Frequency**

- *Routine* → Regular occurrence; solved by established rules.
    
    *Example*: Frequent power outage – backup generator starts automatically.
    
- *Exceptional* → Occasional, unexpected; requires managerial experience.
    
    *Example*: Sudden entry of a global MNC disrupting the local market.
    

**B. By Urgency**

- *Urgent* → Must be solved immediately.
    
    *Example*: Production line breakdown halts output.
    
- *Non‑urgent* → Can take time for consultation and careful analysis.

**C. By Impact**

- *Overall Impact* → Affects entire organisation (e.g., fall in overall profits).
- *Partial Impact* → Affects only a segment or unit (e.g., single product line loss).

**D. By Source**

- *Technical* → Machines, materials, production methods.
- *Human* → Behavioural: motivation, dissatisfaction, conflict.

---

### **5.3 Problem‑Solving Strategies (Judith G. Bulin)**

- *Separate the person from the problem* → Focus on the issue, not blame.
- *Listen carefully* → Hear both what is said and what is avoided; ask clarifying questions.
- *Get as much information as possible* → Identify all issues; consult involved parties.
- *Explore alternative solutions* → Ask those close to the problem for suggestions; solutions from them face less resistance.
- *Decide what to do* → Secure agreement on the next steps; choose an acceptable alternative.
- *Create accountability* → Define who does what, by when.
- *Monitor progress* → Check if the plan works; be flexible to adjust.
- *Give credit where due* → Recognise contributions of those involved.

---

### **5.4 Crisis Handling**

- *Crisis* → A serious, high‑intensity problem threatening the organisation's ability to function.
- *Causes*: lack of planning, ignoring problems, reluctance to deal with conflict.

**Phases of Crisis Handling:**

1. *Warning* → Detect early signals; regular monitoring, open communication.
2. *Preparation & Prevention* → Search for potential problems; train staff, drill responses.
3. *Damage Limitation* → Contain the crisis through careful analysis and preparation.
4. *Recovery* → Address the problem quickly; bring operations back to normal.
5. *Organisational Learning* → Analyse why it happened; prevent recurrence.

**Steps in Crisis Management:**

- *Anticipating* → Understand possible sources by analysing work and people.
- *Contingency Planning* → Develop specific "Plan B" actions, including warning signals.
- *Practicing* → Test the contingency plan through drills so it works in a real crisis.
    
    *Example*: A bank regularly tests its cyber‑attack response plan to ensure staff know exactly how to react if systems are breached.
