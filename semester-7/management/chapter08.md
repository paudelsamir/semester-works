## **1. Concept of Motivation**

- *Motivation* → An inner state that energises, activates, and directs behaviour toward achieving organisational goals.
- As a *management function*, it is the process of inspiring people to take required actions by satisfying their needs.
- *Key idea*: Motivation answers *"Why do people do what they do?"* — it is the psychological force that drives effort.

**Importance of Motivation:**

- *Improves productivity* → Willing workers produce more.
- *Creates willingness* → Employees go beyond the bare minimum.
- *Develops positive attitude* → Reduces absenteeism, grievances.
- *Optimal use of resources* → Motivated people minimise waste.
- *Helps manage change* → Motivated employees embrace new methods.

*Understanding* → A motivated employee is like a self‑charging battery. External inputs (rewards, recognition) help, but the energy comes from within.

## **2. Theories of Motivation**

### **2.1 Maslow's Need Hierarchy Theory**

- *Abraham Maslow* arranged human needs in a five‑level pyramid, from lower to higher order.
- *Core principle*: A satisfied need no longer motivates. Once one level is substantially fulfilled, the next higher level emerges as the dominant motivator.

**The Five Levels (Bottom → Top):**

1. *Physiological Needs* → Basic survival: food, water, shelter, sleep.
    
    *Workplace example*: Adequate salary to afford living expenses, rest breaks.
    
2. *Safety Needs* → Security, stability, protection from harm.
    
    *Workplace example*: Job security, pension plans, safe working conditions, health insurance.
    
3. *Social Needs* → Belonging, love, friendship, acceptance.
    
    *Workplace example*: Friendly colleagues, team‑based projects, office social events.
    
4. *Esteem Needs* → Recognition, status, self‑respect, achievement.
    
    *Workplace example*: Job title, praise from boss, award for "Employee of the Month."
    
5. *Self‑Actualisation Needs* → Becoming the best one can be; growth, creativity, reaching full potential.
    
    *Workplace example*: Challenging assignments, opportunities for innovation, personal development programmes.
    

*Managerial implication* → Identify which need level an employee is at, and offer rewards that match it. For example, a person struggling to pay rent (physiological) will not be motivated by a "best‑idea" award (esteem).

---

### **2.2 Herzberg's Motivation‑Hygiene (Two‑Factor) Theory**

- *Frederick Herzberg* extended Maslow's work and split job factors into two categories:

| ***Hygiene Factors (Dissatisfiers)*** | ***Motivators (Satisfiers)*** |
| --- | --- |
| Company policies & administration | Achievement |
| Supervision (technical quality) | Recognition |
| Relationship with supervisor | The work itself (interesting, challenging) |
| Working conditions | Responsibility |
| Salary & benefits | Advancement (promotion) |
| Job security | Personal growth |

- *Hygiene factors* → Do not motivate when present, but their *absence* causes dissatisfaction. They are maintenance factors; meeting them only prevents unhappiness.
    
    *Example*: A clean, air‑conditioned office will not make you jump with joy, but a leaking, sweltering room will make you miserable.
    
- *Motivators* → Intrinsic factors that truly drive performance and satisfaction. When present, they create positive motivation.
    
    *Example*: A meaningful project that challenges your skills, public recognition of a job well done.
    

*Core implication* → To truly motivate, fix hygiene first (pay fairly, provide decent conditions), then enrich the job with motivators (give responsibility, meaningful work, growth opportunities).

## **3. Reward System to Motivate Performance**

- *Reward System* → The formal and informal mechanisms by which performance is defined, evaluated, and rewarded. It includes rules, procedures, and standards for allocating compensation and benefits.
- A sound reward system positively impacts willingness, performance, and retention.

### **3.1 Types of Rewards**

**A. Financial vs. Non‑financial**

- *Financial* → Directly monetary: salary, wages, commission, bonuses, allowances.
    
    *Example*: A 15% annual bonus for meeting sales targets.
    
- *Non‑financial* → Not convertible into cash; based on culture and behaviour: praise, flexible hours, career development, a desirable job title.
    
    *Example*: "Employee of the Month" parking spot, public recognition at a company meeting.
    

**B. Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic**

- *Intrinsic (Internal)* → Derived from the job itself; personal satisfaction.
    
    *Sources*: Job design, job enlargement, job enrichment, empowerment, autonomy.
    
    *Example*: A designer who feels a deep sense of pride and accomplishment after solving a complex UX problem.
    
- *Extrinsic (External)* → Visible, tangible rewards given by others.
    
    *Sources*: Salary, bonus, allowances, promotion, authority.
    
    *Example*: A promotion to Senior Manager with a corner office.
    

### **3.2 Features of an Effective Reward System**

- *Transparent* → Clear criteria; employees understand how rewards are earned.
- *Based on needs* → Recognises what employees value (not all want the same thing).
- *Equitable* → Fair and consistent; avoids discrimination.
- *Related to performance* → Rewards are linked to actual output or behaviour.
- *Economic* → Affordable for the organisation.
- *Variety in rewards* → Mix of financial, non‑financial, intrinsic, and extrinsic.
- *Reinforcement‑based* → Timely rewards that immediately follow desired behaviour strengthen it.

## **4. Motivation through Employee Participation**

*Participatory management* → A philosophy and practice where employees are actively involved in management functions like planning, decision‑making, organizing, and communication. It enhances dignity, respect, honour, and job satisfaction.

### **4.1 Quality of Work Life (QWL)**

- *QWL* → The quality of the relationship between employees and their total working environment.
- *Goal* → Integrate socio‑psychological needs of people with organisational processes and the existing socio‑cultural environment.
- *Components often include*: fair pay, safe conditions, opportunity to use skills, growth prospects, work‑life balance, participative decision‑making.
- *Example*: A company introduces flexible working hours, sets up a collaborative workspace, and creates an employee‑suggestion panel to influence canteen menus and break schedules. This improves the overall *quality of work life*.
- *Effect*: Higher satisfaction, reduced turnover, stronger commitment.

### **4.2 Self‑Managed Teams**

- *Definition* → Autonomous, self‑contained work units that have the authority to implement solutions and bear responsibility for their actions.
- *Key characteristics*:
    - Members collectively decide on work allocation, schedules, and methods.
    - Measure their own performance using self‑set standards (service quality, delivery targets, productivity, cost control).
    - Minimal direct supervision; the team manages itself.
- *Example*: A software development squad that decides its own sprint goals, divides tasks among members, and reviews each other's code without a traditional manager directing every step.
- *Motivational impact*: Being involved in decision‑making gives a sense of ownership and satisfaction, which fuels intrinsic motivation. Employees feel trusted and empowered.
